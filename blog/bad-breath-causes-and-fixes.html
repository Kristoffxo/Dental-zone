Bad breath: what actually causes it, and what actually fixes it

Almost nobody raises this with us directly. It usually comes out near the end of an appointment, phrased as a question about something else.

2026-08-19·2 min read

It is nearly always the mouth, not the stomach

The most common belief we hear is that bad breath comes from the stomach. In the overwhelming majority of cases it does not. Roughly nine times in ten the cause is inside the mouth, and it is bacteria producing sulphur compounds — the same family of chemicals responsible for the smell of rotten eggs.

That is genuinely good news, because a cause inside the mouth is a cause we can find and treat.

The tongue is the usual culprit

The back of the tongue has a rough surface that traps food debris, dead cells and bacteria in a soft coating. Most people brush their teeth diligently and never touch it.

Clean your tongue every morning — a tongue scraper is better than a toothbrush for this, and cheap. Work from as far back as you can comfortably reach, forwards, four or five times, rinsing between strokes. For a great many people this alone solves the problem within a week.

The other real causes

  • Gum disease. Bacteria in the pockets around the teeth produce exactly the compounds that smell. If your gums bleed when you brush, this is likely a factor and it will not resolve on its own.
  • A dry mouth. Saliva is the mouth's natural cleaner. Anything that reduces it — dehydration, breathing through your mouth, several common medications — makes breath worse. This is why it is nearly always worst first thing in the morning.
  • Decay, or an old broken filling. A cavity is a sheltered space that traps food and bacteria.
  • Food. Onion, garlic and spices are absorbed into the bloodstream and released through the lungs, so brushing does not touch them. That version passes on its own.
  • Smoking and tobacco. Both directly, and by causing gum disease and dry mouth.

Why mouthwash disappoints

Most mouthwash masks the smell rather than removing the cause, and the effect fades within an hour. Strongly alcoholic ones can make things worse over time by drying the mouth, which is the exact condition bacteria prefer.

If you use one, choose an alcohol-free antibacterial rinse and treat it as a supplement to cleaning, never as a replacement.

What to do, in order

  • Clean your tongue daily.
  • Clean between your teeth — brushing reaches roughly three-fifths of each tooth's surface.
  • Drink more water than you think you need.
  • Get a professional cleaning if it has been more than six months.

If you have done all four properly for a month and it persists, come in. Persistent bad breath with a genuinely clean mouth occasionally points to a sinus problem, reflux, or uncontrolled diabetes — and that is worth identifying.

Common questions

Why can I not smell my own bad breath?

Because you are continuously exposed to it, and the brain filters out constant smells. Licking the back of your wrist, letting it dry for ten seconds and smelling it gives a rough indication. Asking someone you trust is more reliable.

Does bad breath come from the stomach?

Very rarely. Around nine in ten cases originate in the mouth itself. Digestive causes exist but are uncommon, and they are worth investigating only once the mouth has been properly examined and ruled out.
