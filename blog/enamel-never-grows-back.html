Your enamel never grows back

It is the hardest substance your body makes. It is also the only part of you that, once lost, is gone permanently.

2026-08-19·2 min read

Two facts that only matter together

Enamel is the hardest tissue in the human body — harder than bone. That part is well known.

The part that matters more: enamel contains no living cells. Bone heals because living cells rebuild it. Skin heals for the same reason. Enamel has none, so it has no mechanism to repair itself. Every bit you lose, you have lost for good.

What this changes in practice

  • A cavity is permanent damage, not a temporary illness. We can clean it out and fill the hole. Nobody can regrow what decayed.
  • Fillings are repairs, and repairs have a lifespan. A filled tooth needs watching for the rest of its life, because decay can restart at the margins.
  • Sensitivity is often enamel loss, not a passing phase. Once it is thin enough for cold to reach the dentine underneath, it does not thicken again.

What actually wears it away

Decay is only one route. The commoner and quieter one is acid erosion, which dissolves enamel chemically across years:

  • Frequent soft drinks, packaged juice and lemon water — the acid does this, independently of sugar
  • Sipping anything acidic slowly through the day, which keeps the mouth acidic for hours
  • Reflux, which brings stomach acid up at night
  • Brushing hard with a stiff brush, which scrubs away softened enamel

One useful habit: wait about thirty minutes after anything acidic before brushing. Enamel is temporarily softened straight afterwards, and brushing in that window removes more of it than it protects. Rinse with plain water instead, then brush later.

Remineralisation — what fluoride can and cannot do

Fluoride helps genuinely, but not in the way most people assume. It can re-harden enamel that has been weakened but not yet broken through — an early white-spot lesion can be arrested and strengthened.

What it cannot do is rebuild a hole. Once decay has broken the surface, it needs a filling. This is exactly why the timing of a checkup decides how much treatment you need.

Why six months is the number

Early decay is invisible to you and painless. It is visible to us, and at that stage it is often reversible with fluoride and a change of habit. Left for two years, the same tooth needs a filling. Left longer, a root canal and a crown.

The interval is not arbitrary — it is roughly how long it takes decay to move from a stage we can reverse to a stage we can only repair.

Common questions

Can enamel grow back or be regenerated?

No. Enamel has no living cells, so it cannot repair itself the way bone or skin can. Fluoride can re-harden enamel that is weakened but still intact; once the surface is broken, the tooth needs a filling.

Is tooth sensitivity a sign of enamel loss?

Often, yes. When enamel thins, the dentine beneath it — which connects to the nerve — is more exposed, so cold and sweet register sharply. Sensitivity can have other causes too, including gum recession and cracks, which is why it is worth having looked at rather than managed with toothpaste indefinitely.

Should I brush immediately after a soft drink?

No. Enamel is temporarily softened by acid, and brushing in that window scrubs some of it away. Rinse with plain water and brush about thirty minutes later.
