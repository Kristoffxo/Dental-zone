Cavities in children: what causes them and what actually prevents them

Most childhood cavities are caused by something far more ordinary than sweets, and prevented by something simpler than most parents expect.

2026-08-19·2 min read

It is frequency, not quantity

The common belief is that sugar causes cavities. More precisely: how often sugar reaches the teeth causes cavities.

Every time a child eats something sweet, the mouth turns acidic for roughly twenty to thirty minutes while saliva neutralises it. A child who eats a whole bar of chocolate in one sitting has one acid attack. A child who nurses a sweet drink or nibbles biscuits across an afternoon can have eight or ten. The second child eats less sugar and gets far more decay.

This is the single most useful thing we tell parents. If nothing else changes, cluster sweets with meals instead of spreading them across the day.

The bottle at bedtime

A bottle of milk or juice given to settle a child to sleep is the classic cause of early decay in very young children. Saliva flow drops during sleep, so the liquid sits against the front teeth for hours with nothing washing it away.

If a bottle is needed to settle, make it plain water. If milk is unavoidable, wipe the front teeth with a clean cloth afterwards.

Why milk teeth matter, even though they fall out

This is the most common misconception we hear. Milk teeth are not disposable:

  • They hold space for the adult teeth. Losing one early lets the neighbours drift in, and the adult tooth arrives with nowhere to go — which is how many children end up needing braces they would not otherwise have needed.
  • Decay in a milk tooth can reach the developing adult tooth sitting directly above it.
  • A child with toothache does not sleep, eat, or concentrate at school properly.

What actually prevents cavities

  • Brushing twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste. A smear for under-threes, a pea-sized amount after that.
  • Brush their teeth for them until about seven. Younger children genuinely do not have the hand control, however willing they are. Let them try first, then you do it properly.
  • Do not rinse with water after brushing. Spit, and leave the fluoride on the teeth. Rinsing washes away most of the benefit.
  • Sealants on the biting surfaces of new adult molars — a quick, painless coating that fills the deep grooves where decay usually starts.

When to bring them in

The first visit is best around the first birthday, or when the first teeth appear. Nothing much happens at that appointment, and that is the point — the child learns that the dental chair is unremarkable. Children whose first visit happens because something already hurts are far more likely to be frightened of dentists for years.

Common questions

Do cavities in milk teeth need filling if they will fall out anyway?

Usually yes. It depends how close the tooth is to being lost naturally, but an untreated cavity can cause pain, infection, and damage to the adult tooth forming above it. Losing a milk tooth early also frequently causes crowding later.

At what age should a child first see a dentist?

Around the first birthday, or whenever the first teeth come through. The early visits are mostly about familiarity — a child comfortable in the chair before anything is wrong is far easier to treat when something eventually is.
